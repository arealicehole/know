## Claims

### C1 — CIA/DLI Method: Scream & Scribble (Multi-Sensory Vocabulary Drill)
**Statement:** The DLI (Defense Language Institute, Monterey CA) uses a technique called "Scream and Scribble" where the entire class shouts a new word in unison while rapidly scribbling it on paper. This activates visual, auditory, and kinesthetic/motor channels simultaneously, enabling 20–80 new words per day.
**Evidence refs:** E1, E2
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C2 — CIA/DLI Method: ISO (Iso-Immersion) Simulations
**Statement:** DLIFLC runs "iso-immersion" (isolation/immersion) events — 1 to 3 days off-post, conducted exclusively in the target language, where English is strictly forbidden. Students engage in realistic role-play scenarios (hiring interpreters, resolving traffic accidents, calling travel agencies). Instructors play "aggressors" who are intentionally impatient to stress-test the student's language under pressure. The goal is adaptability over accuracy.
**Evidence refs:** E3, E4
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C3 — CIA/DLI Method: PSI (Progressive Skill Integration)
**Statement:** Developed in 1987, Progressive Skill Integration treats the four language skills (listening, reading, speaking, writing) as a single integrated loop. For any 2-minute source material, the student performs a 4-round cycle: (1) listen with no subtitles, (2) read the transcript and compare, (3) close the text and retell in own words, (4) transcribe own recorded speech. This "integrates" language into a functional tool rather than isolated facts.
**Evidence refs:** E5
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C4 — FSI Time-to-Professional-Working-Proficiency (ILR 3)
**Statement:** The FSI (US State Department) categorizes languages by difficulty for native English speakers:
- **Category I** (e.g., French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese): 552–690 class hours (24–30 weeks)
- **Category II** (e.g., German, Indonesian, Swahili): ~828 class hours (~36 weeks)
- **Category III** (e.g., Russian, Hindi, Thai, Hebrew): ~1,012 class hours (~44 weeks)
- **Category IV** (e.g., Arabic, Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, Korean): ~2,200 class hours (~88 weeks)

A "full-time" week at FSI is ~23 class hours + 17 hours self-study (~40 hours/week).
**Evidence refs:** E6, E7, E8
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C5 — CIA/ILI Operational Fluency Timeline
**Statement:** The CIA's Intelligence Language Institute (ILI) targets "operational fluency" — the ability to speak with a regional accent and use idiomatic vernacular to blend in — in as little as 6 months for Category I languages, and 6–11 months across all categories. Officers are taught to sound local, not just correct.
**Evidence refs:** E9, E10
**Confidence:** medium (secondary sources, no primary CIA training manual published)
**Status:** partial

### C6 — Comprehensible Input (Krashen's Input Hypothesis)
**Statement:** Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis (1982) — the most-cited theory in second language acquisition — holds that learners acquire language only when they understand messages slightly above their current level ("i+1"). The affective filter hypothesis adds that anxiety blocks acquisition even when input is comprehensible. This is the single most reliable driver of passive understanding (listening + reading).
**Evidence refs:** E11, E12, E13
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C7 — Spaced Repetition (SRS)
**Statement:** Cepeda et al. (2006, *Psychological Science*) reviewed 184 experiments and found distributed (spaced) practice produces roughly 10x higher retention at 1 month vs. massed practice, in the same total study time. Roediger & Karpicke (2006) showed testing yourself (active recall) outperforms re-reading by ~50% on delayed tests. Anki and similar SRS apps combine both effects: they test you and schedule reviews at the optimal moment.
**Evidence refs:** E14, E15
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C8 — Shadowing
**Statement:** Shadowing (speaking along with native audio at near-native pace, 2–3 words behind) is a technique originally developed to train simultaneous interpreters. A 2025 systematic review (Whitworth & Rose, 44 studies) found consistently positive evidence for speaking fluency, prosody/rhythm, and comprehensibility. Evidence for individual phoneme accuracy is mixed. Gains plateau after 4–5 repetitions of the same passage (Shiki et al., 2010); rotating to new material is more effective. Lower-proficiency learners see larger listening-comprehension gains.
**Evidence refs:** E16, E17
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C9 — Pushed Output (Swain's Output Hypothesis)
**Statement:** Merrill Swain (1985) argued that input alone is insufficient for accurate production. Learners must be "pushed" to produce language under conditions that force precision — noticing the gap between what they want to say and what they can say. Research shows pushed output increases language-related episodes (LREs) and improves accuracy, though the effect on fluency is less consistent. The "Swain gap" is the well-documented phenomenon where passive understanding outpaces production ability.
**Evidence refs:** E18, E19
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C10 — Immersion (Study Abroad)
**Statement:** A 2024 multi-level meta-analysis (Tseng et al., 42 studies, 283 effect sizes) found study abroad programs produce a medium-to-large effect (g = 0.87) on L2 acquisition. The effect is strongest for lower-proficiency learners, and is enhanced when learners take both formal and content-based courses while living with host families. Immersion benefits fluency (speed, fluidity) more than grammatical accuracy.
**Evidence refs:** E20, E21
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C11 — Deliberate Practice
**Statement:** Ericsson's research (1993) showed that expert-level improvement comes not from more hours but from deliberate, focused effort targeting current weaknesses, at the edge of ability, with feedback. Applied to language: one hour of targeted conversation with correction produces more acquisition than five hours of comfortable small talk. Deliberate practice is the key to breaking through the intermediate plateau.
**Evidence refs:** E22
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C12 — Extensive Reading
**Statement:** Paul Nation's research (Victoria University) shows that when learners know 95–98% of the words on a page, vocabulary and grammar patterns are absorbed through sheer exposure volume. Waring & Takaki (2003) found learners acquire 15–30% of new words simply from reading a 700-page graded novel without studying any words explicitly. A 2024 meta-analysis confirmed positive short-term effects across all language domains, with larger effects when text choice is limited and accountability is included.
**Evidence refs:** E23, E24
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C13 — The Minimal Effective Daily Stack
**Statement:** The most efficient daily stack, based on convergent evidence, is:
- 30 min comprehensible input (listening/reading at i+1)
- 15 min spaced-repetition flashcard review (cloze/sentence cards)
- 15–20 min output (shadowing, AI conversation, or tutor session)

This 60–90 minute daily block, run consistently for 90 days, produces measurable progress to CEFR A2–B1 (daily conversational level) for Category I languages.
**Evidence refs:** E25, E26, E27
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C14 — The 90-Day Phased Plan
**Statement:** A realistic 90-day fast-track plan has three phases:
- **Days 1–30 (Habit foundation):** 30 min/day (10 input, 10 SRS, 10 output). 10–15 new cloze cards/day. 1 tutor session/week. Target: 150+ cards active, follow a beginner podcast.
- **Days 31–60 (Volume expansion):** 30–45 min/day. Move to native content with subtitles. 20 new cards/day. 2–3 speaking sessions/week. Target: 300+ cards active, read short native articles.
- **Days 61–90 (Fluency push):** Output-dominant sessions. 4–5 speaking sessions/week. Shadowing daily. Target: hold a 5-minute conversation; record Day 90 audio and compare to Day 1.
**Evidence refs:** E25, E26, E27
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C15 — Frequency List Priority
**Statement:** The 1,000 most frequent words in any language cover roughly 75–90% of everyday speech. Early weeks should prioritize depth with a small high-frequency core before breadth. Once vocabulary reaches ~1,500–2,000 words, comprehensible input becomes significantly more productive.
**Evidence refs:** E25, E28
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

### C16 — The "Affective Filter" and Low-Stress Practice
**Statement:** Krashen's Affective Filter Hypothesis holds that anxiety, low motivation, and low self-confidence block acquisition even when input is comprehensible. Practical implications: use AI conversation partners to reduce social anxiety; narrate to yourself; record and review rather than perform live; never miss twice (one missed day is a rest, two is a broken habit).
**Evidence refs:** E11, E13, E26
**Confidence:** high
**Status:** supported

## Gaps / Unresolved Questions

1. **No published CIA training manual.** The CIA does not publish its ILI training materials. The "Scream and Scribble" and "ISO" descriptions come from secondary sources (fluentcards.org, legalclarity.org, DLIFLC official website) and Olly Richards' YouTube documentary about DLI. The core methods are well-corroborated, but the exact curricula are classified.
2. **Individual aptitude variation.** The FSI numbers are averages. The DLAB (Defense Language Aptitude Battery) test measures an individual's aptitude for pattern recognition in a new language. A high-aptitude learner may reach ILR 3 in half the FSI time; a low-aptitude learner may take double. The test uses a made-up language to avoid cheating.
3. **Long-term retention of shadowing gains.** Most shadowing studies use controlled, scripted tasks and few include delayed post-tests beyond a few weeks. Whether shadowing gains persist over months without continued practice is unclear.
4. **Immersion accuracy vs. fluency.** Immersion clearly improves fluency and speed, but grammatical accuracy improves more slowly. A 10-month immersion study (Chinese → English, UK) found significant speed gains but no significant accuracy improvement, particularly for complex grammatical structures.
5. **Optimal daily time for non-immersion learners.** The 60–90 minute daily stack is a reasonable target, but the minimum effective dose for meaningful progress is not precisely established. Below 30 minutes/day, visible momentum is rare. Above 2 hours/day, burnout and dropout rates increase.

## Recommended Next Actions

1. **Pick a language** and look up its FSI category to set realistic timeline expectations.
2. **Set up Anki** (or similar SRS app) with cloze-deletion sentence cards from a frequency list of the top 1,000 words.
3. **Choose a comprehensible input source** at your current level:
   - Beginner: Dreaming Spanish, Comprehensible Spanish/French, graded readers
   - Intermediate: native TV shows with native-language subtitles, podcasts
   - Advanced: native news, novels, unscripted conversation
4. **Add one output channel**:
   - Shadowing: 2-minute clip daily, 4–6 reps, rotate material weekly
   - AI conversation: 15–20 min daily (Sparring, AI tutor, or iTalki)
   - Live tutor: 1–5 sessions/week depending on phase
5. **Record Day 1 audio.** Compare to Day 90. The delta is the proof.
6. **Protect the daily-ness.** A short session most days beats a marathon once a week. Never miss twice.
